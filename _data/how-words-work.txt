A sentence is a chain--each link holds the next--and the links are not all of the same kind. Some
carry a meaning of their own: rain, falls, cold, river. Others carry almost none, and would be
hard to draw or point at: the, of, and, to, is. Take the second kind away and what remains is a
list. "Rain river stone" is three things standing next to each other. "The rain fills the river
and the river moves the stone" is an account of something that happened. The words that made the
difference are the ones that mean the least on their own.

It is worth asking what a word is, because the answer is less obvious than it looks. A speaker
does not pause between words. Speech is continuous, and the ear does the dividing. Ask where one
word ends in a spoken line and the answer comes from what the listener already knows, not from
anything present in the sound. On the page the divisions are marked, but the marking is a
convention and the convention has ragged edges. Is "riverbed" one word or two? English writes
that one closed up, writes "river bank" open, and allows "riverside" either way in older books.
The thing described is the same in all three cases: a thing and the thing beside it. What differs
is where a printer decided to put a space.

The space itself had to be invented. Latin and Greek were written for centuries without it,
letter after letter across the line, and a reader worked out the divisions aloud as he went. Word
spacing spreads through western manuscripts ca. 700 and is general by 1000. It took hold because
it made silent reading possible: the eye can take in a spaced line at a glance, while an unspaced
line has to be sounded out before it can be understood. Nothing about the language changed. What
changed was the amount of work the reader had to do, and the space is doing that work for anyone
reading this line now.

Words that name things are the easiest to collect. Stone, leaf, water, frost, root, wing, sand,
seed, cloud, salt, bark, tide. Each one points at something a person could carry or stand in, and
the pointing is the whole of what it does. Names come in ranks. "Oak" names a kind of tree,
"tree" names a kind of plant, "plant" names a kind of living thing, and each step upward covers
more ground and says less about any of it. A speaker who wants to be exact goes down the ranks; a
speaker who wants to be brief goes up them. Neither direction is more correct, and most sentences
move between them without anyone noticing.

A name in English changes shape when there is more than one of the thing. One stone, two stones.
One leaf, two leaves. One root, two roots. The change is small, it is carried at the end of the
word, and it is not optional: "two stone" is wrong in a way a listener hears before he can say
why. Some names refuse the change altogether. Water, sand, snow, and dust have no plural, because
they are not counted but measured; a speaker says "much sand" and "many stones" and does not mix
the two. The line between the countable and the measured is not drawn by nature. It is drawn by
the language, and another language draws it in a different place.

A word is often built out of smaller pieces, and the pieces can be recognised even inside a word
that has never been seen before. Take "frost": add an ending and it becomes "frosted"; add a
different one and it becomes "frosty"; put a piece in front and it becomes "defrost". None of
those endings means anything by itself, and none of them can stand alone on a page, but each does
a definite job and does the same job wherever it turns up. This is why a reader who meets
"unrootedness" for the first time can work out what it has to mean without looking it up: un-,
root, -ed, -ness, four pieces each doing a known job in a known order. The pieces are far fewer
than the words, and that is the point of having them. A language that built every word whole
would need a separate name for every shade of every idea. A language that builds words out of
parts can make the shade it needs on the spot and still be understood.

Words that name what happens carry a different load. Falls, froze, grows, will break. Besides
saying what occurred, they say when, and they say it either by changing their own shape or by
taking a helper in front of them. "The river freezes" and "the river froze" differ by one vowel,
and that vowel is the difference between now and over. Some of these words change by rule and
some by inheritance: walked, worked, waited, but froze, grew, broke. The irregular ones are the
old and common ones, which is not an accident. A form keeps its irregularity in proportion to how
often it is used, because a form that is heard every day is learned as itself and never gets
flattened into the pattern.

The joints of a sentence are its conjunctions, and there are surprisingly few of them. And, but,
or, so, for, yet, because, although, if, while, until, unless. They carry no picture at all, and a
reader asked to draw "although" has nothing to draw, and yet they decide what the sentence is
claiming. "The frost came AND the seed split" reports two events. "The frost came SO the seed
split" reports a cause. "The frost came BUT the seed held" reports a resistance. The two events
are unchanged in all three lines. Only the joint moved, and the joint turns out to be where the
meaning of the whole was kept.

Turning a statement into a question, or into a denial, takes machinery of its own. "The ice held"
is denied by putting a word inside it: "the ice did not hold", where a helper appears that was not
there before and the main word gives up its ending. It is questioned by moving that same helper to
the front: "did the ice hold?" English does both jobs with one small set of helpers--do, did,
does, is, was, has, can, will--and the same handful serves throughout. Other languages need
neither arrangement. Some mark a question with a single particle at the end of the line and change
nothing else; some mark it only with the pitch of the voice, and in writing only with the mark at
the end. What all of them share is that the machinery is small, regular, and used constantly,
which is the usual description of the parts of a language that are hardest to notice.

A second small set of words fixes position. On, under, over, through, beside, before, after,
between, against, along. "The moss grows on the stone" and "the moss grows under the stone" use
the same four other words and describe two different places. Most of these began as words about
space and were then pressed into service for time: before the rain and before the door, after the
frost and after the gate. The extension is so old that a speaker no longer feels it as one, and
asking whether "before" is really about space or really about time gets no answer, because it has
been about both for as long as there is any record of it.

Position carries information in English that other languages carry in endings. "The wind moved
the sand" and "the sand moved the wind" contain exactly the same five words and state opposite
things, and nothing but the order tells them apart. This makes English word order rigid where it
matters and loose where it does not. The doer comes first and the thing done comes last, but a
phrase about time or place can sit at either end: "in the morning the frost lifted" and "the
frost lifted in the morning" are both ordinary. Move that same phrase into the middle and the
line stops sounding like speech and starts sounding like verse.

Three of the commonest words in the language do nothing except tell a reader whether a thing has
been mentioned before. A, an, the. "A river runs below the hill" introduces the river. "The river
runs below the hill" assumes the reader already has it in hand. Nothing is added to the river
either way, and a great many languages do without the distinction entirely. In English it is not
optional, and a sentence that drops it does not become ambiguous so much as foreign.

Words that narrow a name stand in front of it: cold water, dry sand, black stone, thin ice. They
can be stacked, and when they are stacked the order is fixed by a rule that no speaker is ever
taught. Size comes before age, age before colour, colour before material, so "a small old grey
stone wall" is ordinary and "a grey old small stone wall" is not. Ask a speaker to state the rule
and he will not produce it. Give him a list in the wrong order and he will correct it without
hesitating. This is the ordinary condition of grammar: it is known, and it is not known about.

A short set of words stands in for a name already given. It, they, this, that, one. Their whole
usefulness lies in being empty. "The pine grew beside the stream until it fell" costs less than
repeating the pine, and a reader has no trouble supplying what is meant. But the emptiness has a
price. In "the pine grew beside the stream until it dried", the same small word now points at the
stream instead, and the only thing that decided the matter was the verb at the end. Change one
word in the last position and a word in the middle changes what it refers to. No mark anywhere on
the line records that the change took place.

Some marks on a line are not words at all. The period, the comma, the colon, the semicolon, the
question mark, the pair of quotation marks. They belong to writing rather than to speech; a
speaker uses pitch and pause where a writer uses these. Their work is real. "The rain stopped,
and the wind rose" and "the rain stopped and the wind rose" differ only in the size of the break,
which is a small thing. "What remains, is water" and "what remains is water" differ in whether
the line sounds broken, which is not small at all. And one mark can turn a statement into a
question. Consider: you know the answer. Now consider: do you know the answer?

Every letter in the alphabet has two forms, and the second form carries no sound of its own.
"Stone" and "stone" are the same word said the same way, and what separates them is only where
they stand: one begins a line, the other does not. The capital does other work besides. It marks a
name, so that "frost" and "Frost" can be a thing and a place. It can mark emphasis, when a whole
word is set that way. What it never marks is a difference in the word itself, which means the two
forms are one word wearing two coats--and also means that anything counting the forms of words on
a page will count them as two unless it is told otherwise. The two-form system is not universal.
Several writing systems have one form per sign and lose nothing by it, and the readers of those
systems do not report any difficulty telling a name from a common word.

One mark does two unrelated jobs, and it causes more trouble than all the others together. The
apostrophe marks possession: the river's mouth, the tree's shadow, the season's end. It also
marks a letter that has been left out: "it's" standing for "it is", "don't" for "do not". The two
uses are identical on the page, and a reader tells them apart from the sentence around them,
which is to say he tells them apart from nothing that is written down. Consider a line that uses
the first sense twice: "The river's mouth was wide, and the tree's shadow fell across it." Now
consider "the stone's edge" beside "the stone's cold". Those are the same three characters doing
two different jobs, and only the words that follow decide which job it is.

The same string of letters can carry two readings, and a reader usually chooses one without
noticing that a choice was available. "The old man the boats" stops most readers on the first
pass, because "man" is being used as a verb and "old" as a name for a group of people. "Time
flies like an arrow" has four readings and only one of them is about time. Ambiguity of this kind
is not a defect. It is what happens when a small set of forms is made to cover an unlimited set of
situations, and the alternative--a distinct form for every meaning--would need a vocabulary that
nobody could finish learning.

Sometimes the meaning of a phrase cannot be got from the meanings of its words. "The frost broke"
can be worked out from the parts. "It broke the ice" cannot, if what is meant is that someone made
a difficult conversation easier. The words are ordinary and the grammar is ordinary, and yet the
whole is something a reader either knows or does not. Every language is full of these, and they are
learned the way single words are learned, one at a time and by exposure. This is a real limit on
the idea that a sentence is assembled out of its words. Most sentences are. Some are assembled out
of longer pieces that merely happen to be made of words, and the joins inside those pieces are not
available for taking apart.

Count the words in a long enough passage and a pattern appears that has nothing to do with the
passage. A few words are everywhere: the, of, and, to, a, in, is, that. A great many appear once
and never again. In ordinary English the commonest word will usually account for about one word in
sixteen, the second commonest about half as often, the third about a third as often, and the
series continues in that shape whatever the subject happens to be. The words at the top of the
list carry the least meaning and the words at the bottom carry the most. Both facts follow from
the same cause: a word that can be used anywhere ends up being used everywhere.

A language takes words from its neighbours, and English has taken more than most. Anchor, cheese,
and street came in from Latin very early. Sky, root, and egg came in from Norse. Mountain, river,
and forest arrived from French, and in a number of cases sit beside an older word for something
close to the same thing, so that the language ends up holding two names and putting them to
slightly different uses. Sugar, cotton, and lemon came along trade routes from further off, and
their spelling still shows something of the road they travelled. A borrowed word is not felt as
foreign for long. Within a generation or two it takes the ordinary endings, forms an ordinary
plural, and is used freely by speakers who would be surprised to learn it came from anywhere. This
is how a vocabulary grows, and it is also why a list of the words in a language is never complete
for very long.

Short words are common and long words are rare, and the relation is close enough to be useful.
The words a reader meets most often are nearly all of one syllable: the, of, and, to, in, is, it,
that, was, for. Words of four syllables and more turn up a few times a page at most. This is not
an accident of English. A word that is said constantly gets worn down by use, because every
speaker shortens what every speaker already understands, and a word that is said rarely keeps its
full shape because there is no wear on it. The same pressure works on phrases: what starts as a
sentence becomes a phrase, the phrase becomes a word, and the word loses a syllable. Nothing
plans this. It is the sum of a great many speakers each doing the least work that will still be
understood, and the result is a vocabulary sorted by effort, with the cheapest words carrying the
most traffic.

One written form can carry two unrelated words, and a reader separates them without effort from
what stands nearby. A bank is the side of a river and also a place that holds money. Spring is a
season, a source of water, a coil of metal, and a sudden movement. Light is the opposite of dark
and also the opposite of heavy. These are not shades of one meaning; they are separate words that
happen to be spelled alike, and in several cases they arrived in the language from different
directions centuries apart. What resolves them is never the word itself. "The bank was steep"
and "the bank was closed" differ in one adjective, and that one adjective decides which of two
words the reader has just met. A form on its own carries less than it appears to; the sentence
around it supplies the rest, and it supplies it so quickly that a reader does not notice a
decision was made.

Writing and speech have drifted apart, and the drift shows in the spelling. The letters "ough"
stand for six different sounds in though, through, rough, cough, bough, and thorough. A silent
"e" at the end of a word changes the vowel before it: rat and rate, not and note, thin and thine.
None of this was designed. Spelling was fixed by printers at one moment in the history of the
pronunciation, and the pronunciation went on moving afterwards. What is left is a system that
records how the language sounded several centuries ago, and that a child now has to learn as a
list rather than as a rule.

An alphabet is a small answer to a large problem. There are far more words in a language than
anyone could give separate signs to, and there are far more possible words than have yet been
said. But the number of distinct sounds is small--English uses about forty--and every word is a
sequence drawn from that set. So a system that gives one sign to each sound needs a few dozen
signs and can write anything that can be spoken, including words that do not exist yet. This is
the most economical arrangement anyone has found for the purpose, and it appears to have been
arrived at once; every alphabet now in use descends from a single ancestor.

Not every system chose that unit. A sign can stand for a whole word, for a syllable, or for a
single sound, and all three have been used at length and at scale. A word-sign system needs
thousands of signs and a reader who has learned them all, but a page written in it is short, and
it can be read by speakers who do not share a spoken language. A syllable system needs a few
hundred signs. A sound system needs a few dozen and produces the longest written strings of the
three. Each arrangement trades the size of the sign list against the length of the written line,
and there is no setting of that trade which is best for every purpose. Systems in use today mix
all three, and the mixing is not a failure of design. It is the ordinary result of a system that
has to handle very common words quickly and very rare words at all.

Asking how many words a language has turns out to be the wrong question, and it is instructive to
see why. A large English dictionary lists something over half a million entries, but that number
is a decision rather than a measurement. Does a plural count separately from its singular? Does a
past tense? Does a compound that any speaker could build on the spot, or a technical name for one
of the several million known species of insect? Each answer moves the total by a great deal. And
against that half million, an educated adult speaker recognises perhaps thirty or forty thousand
words and uses far fewer in ordinary speech. The gap is not a failing. A speaker does not need the
whole list, because the words that carry the traffic are few and the rest can be worked out from
their parts, looked up, or done without. Any system that has to handle a language faces the same
choice the dictionary maker faces: how large a list to keep, and what to do about everything that
falls outside it.

Names are the hardest case for any such list. A common noun can be learned once and used for the
rest of a life: stone means stone wherever it is met. But names are unlimited and they keep
arriving. Every settlement, every river, every person, every ship and street and mountain carries
one, and new ones are made constantly while old ones fall out of use. No list can hold them, and
unlike ordinary words they cannot be worked out from their parts, because a name is not built out
of meaning. Writing marks them with a capital in English and leaves it at that. Speech does not
mark them at all, and a listener who has not heard a name before takes it in as a shape and waits
for the sentence to say what kind of thing it is. This is the ordinary condition: a form arrives
with no meaning attached, and everything the listener learns about it comes from what surrounds
it.

A dictionary is a list, and every list needs an order. Alphabetical order is the one that was
settled on, and it is worth seeing how arbitrary it is. Nothing about "ash" makes it belong before
"birch" except an agreement about the sequence of the letters, and that agreement is inherited
from the shape of a sign list drawn up long before either word existed. The order does not group
like with like. "Frost" sits beside "frost-bite" and "froth", which have nothing to do with one
another, while "frost" and "ice" are pages apart. What alphabetical order gives up in meaning it
wins back in searching: any word at all can be found by following a rule, without knowing
anything whatever about what it means.

A sentence can be put inside a sentence, and the putting can be repeated. "The stone fell" is one
statement. "The stone that the water loosened fell" holds a second statement inside the first, and
a reader has to keep the opening in mind across the whole of the insertion before the closing
arrives. "The stone that the water that the frost had thawed loosened fell" is built by the same
rule applied one more time, and it is very nearly unreadable, although nothing in it breaks any
rule. This is worth noticing because the limit is not in the grammar. The grammar permits any
depth at all. The limit is in how much of an unfinished structure a reader can hold open at once,
and it turns out to be about two levels. A rule can be unlimited and still be unusable past a
certain point, and the point is set by the reader rather than by the rule.

Move a sentence into another language and something is always left behind. Some of what goes is
sound: the rhythm goes, and any rhyme with it. Some of it is the shape of the grammar, because a
language that marks the doer with an ending does not need the word order that English needs, so a
translation into English has to commit to an order that the original never committed to. And some
of it is simply that two vocabularies cut the world at different joints. A language spoken where
snow lasts eight months of the year will have single words for kinds of snow that a translator
can only render as a phrase, and the phrase is both longer and vaguer than the word it replaces.
A translation can be exact about what was said. It cannot be exact about everything that was
carried.

A great deal of every sentence is never written down. "The rain stopped and the wind rose" does not
say that the second thing happened in the same place as the first, or outdoors, or shortly
afterwards; the reader supplies all of it. "Cold water, then warmer" is not a sentence at all by
any strict account, and is perfectly clear. What makes this work is that a reader arrives already
holding a set of expectations, so a writer has only to mark the places where those expectations
fail. The consequence is that the same line means somewhat different things to readers who arrive
holding different expectations, and no amount of care in the writing removes the dependence. A line
carries less than it appears to. The rest is supplied from outside it, and the supplying leaves no
trace on the page.

What all of this describes is a system that nobody designed. There was no meeting at which the
space between words was adopted, no committee that fixed the order in which adjectives stack, no
decision to give the apostrophe two jobs and let readers sort it out. Each part was arrived at by
many people solving a local problem, and the parts were kept because they worked well enough to
be copied by the next person. The result is regular enough that its rules can be written down, and
irregular enough that the writing down is never finished. A speaker of a language knows nearly all
of it and can state almost none of it, and the distance between those two facts is where the study
of language begins.
